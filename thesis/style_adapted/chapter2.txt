\section{Football Domain Background}
\label{sec:football_background}

Because the methodology proposed in this thesis is grounded in football-specific statistics, this section briefly clarifies the key terms used throughout the work. Readers familiar with the sport can safely skip ahead to Section~\ref{sec:predictive_football}.

A football match is played between two teams of eleven players over two halves of $45$ minutes each, on a rectangular pitch with a goal at each end. One of the two teams plays \emph{at home}, on its own pitch and in front of its own supporters, while the other plays \emph{away}. The systematic performance advantage enjoyed by the home side is referred to as \emph{home advantage} and is a well-documented effect in the football analytics literature. The objective of the game is to score more goals than the opponent. A match can end with a home win, a draw, or an away win.

Several other concepts appear repeatedly in this work. A \emph{shot} is any attempt to score. A \emph{shot on target} is a shot that would have entered the goal if not blocked or saved. \emph{Possession} is usually reported as the percentage of time during which one team controls the ball. A \emph{corner} is awarded when the defending team is the last to touch the ball before it crosses its own goal line outside the goal, and is taken from the corner arc. Corners are typically used as a proxy for sustained attacking pressure. A \emph{penalty kick} is a direct kick from a fixed mark in front of the goal, awarded for fouls committed inside the penalty area. Because of the high conversion rate, penalties are a relatively rare but high-impact event. \emph{Yellow} and \emph{red cards} are disciplinary sanctions. Two yellow cards in the same match are equivalent to a red card, which forces the player off the pitch and reduces the team to ten players for the remainder of the match.

\emph{Rest days} count the number of days between two consecutive matches for the same team and provide a simple but informative proxy for fatigue and schedule density. \emph{Head-to-head} context refers to the recent history of matches between the same two teams, which often captures stylistic factors that aggregate statistics may miss.

Finally, throughout this thesis team strength is summarised by an \emph{Elo rating}, a dynamic ranking system originally introduced for chess by Arpad Elo~\cite{elo1978rating} and now widely applied to team sports. Each team has a rating that is updated after every match according to the difference between the actual and the expected result, where the expected result is computed from the rating gap between the two teams. A team that wins against a stronger opponent gains more rating points than one that wins against a weaker opponent. The difference between the two ratings before a fixture, the so-called \emph{Elo gap}, is one of the most informative pre-match features used in the predictive pipeline of this thesis. The exact football-specific parameters used here (the square-root goal-margin scaling and the dynamic home-field-advantage update introduced by the ClubElo system~\cite{clubelo}) are defined formally in Section~\ref{sec:datasets}.

\section{Predictive Analytics in Football}
\label{sec:predictive_football}

Predictive analytics refers to the use of historical data, statistical methods, and machine learning algorithms to estimate future outcomes~\cite{bertsimas2020predictive}. In football, this usually means forecasting match results, goal production, or the probability that a team will win a game. Because football combines measurable performance signals (such as goals, shots, possession, and rest days) with a high level of uncertainty, it is one of the most challenging sports for predictive modelling~\cite{rodrigues2022prediction,haruna2021predicting,tacspinar2021improvement}.

A major difficulty of football prediction is that match outcomes depend on many factors that interact in non-linear ways. Team quality, home advantage, fatigue, short-term form, tactical approach, and opponent strength are only some of the aspects that must be taken into consideration when attempting to predict the final result of a match. At the same time, football contains a large amount of randomness. A single red card, deflection, penalty, or finishing sequence may alter the outcome of a game in ways that are difficult to fully capture through pre-match variables. As a result, football prediction has a natural performance ceiling when based only on aggregate pre-match statistical data~\cite{rodrigues2022prediction,haruna2021predicting}.

Another challenge is the choice of target formulation. A traditional approach models football as a three-class classification task: home win, draw, or away win. However, draws are often difficult to model consistently because they emerge from many different tactical and statistical scenarios. In practice, they introduce additional ambiguity into the learning problem and often reduce model stability. When draws are kept as a separate class, football result prediction remains difficult, and performance depends strongly on the selected features, the learning setup, and the modelling assumptions~\cite{rodrigues2022prediction,kozak2021heterogeneous}. In addition, earlier studies report that two-class settings, in which the prediction target collapses the away-win and draw outcomes into a single \emph{Not Home Win} class, can outperform three-class settings in football outcome prediction~\cite{tacspinar2021improvement}.

Predictive performance in football also depends heavily on feature engineering. Raw match results alone are usually insufficient to capture the underlying context of a fixture. More informative systems incorporate indicators of team strength, tactical form, recent performance, and match conditions. Research has shown that carefully selected features can substantially improve football prediction performance, especially when contextual signals are combined with tactical statistics~\cite{tacspinar2021improvement,haruna2021predicting}.

As for the modelling approaches to match-result prediction, several machine learning models have been explored in the literature, including regression-based methods, support vector machines, neural networks, Bayesian approaches, and ensemble models~\cite{rodrigues2022prediction,haruna2021predicting,kozak2021heterogeneous}. Logistic regression in particular has been used as a strong and interpretable baseline for football win-probability estimation~\cite{kollar2021predicting}. This suggests that no single algorithm universally dominates the football prediction problem. Instead, model effectiveness depends on the target formulation, the selected features, the competition under study, and the evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Relevant Studies in Football Analytics}
\label{sec:relevant_studies}

There are a number of approaches to predicting football match outcomes. The following review focuses on those that employ machine learning techniques, as they are the most relevant with respect to the current study. The studies were selected because each one isolates a different methodological lever (data structure, feature design, ensemble composition, style of play), and together they cover the spectrum of choices that shape a football outcome predictor.

Rodrigues and Pinto~\cite{rodrigues2022prediction} investigate football match-result prediction on five English Premier League seasons (2013/14 through 2018/19, roughly $1{,}900$ matches), comparing eight supervised classifiers (Random Forest, SVM, XGBoost, k-NN, C5.0, multinomial logistic regression, artificial neural networks, and Naive Bayes) on a feature set that combines player-level skill attributes with rolling match statistics. Their best configuration, a Random Forest reduced to $15$ features, attains $65.26\%$ test accuracy and a positive bookmaker-implied profit margin of $26.78\%$, illustrating that even on a well-studied league a modern ensemble peaks around two-thirds of matches predicted correctly.

Haruna et al.~\cite{haruna2021predicting} also study football outcome prediction through machine learning, with a focus on how the choice of input features affects classification performance. Working on two English Premier League seasons and testing combinations of contextual features (home/away identity, the twenty-two starting players, and goal difference), the authors report a top accuracy of $83.95\%$ using a $k$-nearest-neighbours classifier, illustrating that a richer feature set can substantially raise the prediction ceiling for the same league.

Taspinar et al.~\cite{tacspinar2021improvement} emphasise the importance of selecting effective features for football score prediction, working on six Italian Serie A seasons (2014/15 through 2019/20, $2{,}027$ matches) with an initial pool of $54$ candidate features. After ANOVA-based feature selection retains the most informative $28$ features, a logistic-regression classifier improves its accuracy from $88.85\%$ to $89.63\%$\footnote{Their evaluation protocol differs from the strict rolling-origin chronological holdout used in this thesis, so the comparison is methodological rather than directly numerical.}, showing that careful selection of inputs can yield a small but meaningful gain over using the full feature set.

Kozak and G{\l}owania~\cite{kozak2021heterogeneous} explore heterogeneous ensembles for football result prediction on German Bundesliga data covering ten seasons from 2010/11 to 2019/20. By combining classifiers of different families (decision trees, support vector machines, random forests, and additional probabilistic classifiers) inside a voting-based ensemble, the authors report consistent improvements over the strongest individual classifier, while also illustrating the persistent difficulty of football prediction in balanced and uncertain matches.

Beyond pure result prediction, Ruiz et al.~\cite{ruiz2017leicester} show that strategic and style-of-play features can provide valuable information about football performance and can support recommendation-oriented tools for pre-game preparation. Their case study on Leicester City across the 2015/16 and 2016/17 Premier League seasons uses Expected Goal Value (xG), Expected Save Value (xS), and passing-quality metrics to identify a defensive xGA differential of $-10.7$ goals in the title-winning season (the best across the five preceding EPL seasons), which demonstrates that advanced football analytics can move beyond descriptive summaries and support decision-oriented interpretations of performance.

These five studies are particularly relevant to the present thesis for complementary reasons. Rodrigues and Pinto motivate the treatment of football prediction as a noisy probabilistic task, which justifies the calibration-oriented evaluation adopted later in this work. Haruna et al.\ and Taspinar et al.\ provide direct empirical evidence that feature design and target formulation matter as much as the choice of classifier, which is mirrored here through engineered pre-match features and the binary Home-Win-or-Not formulation. Kozak and G{\l}owania support the broader claim that no single algorithm dominates and that model selection must be aligned with the specific purpose of the system, which in turn justifies the deliberate split, in this thesis, between the best \emph{predictive} model and the best \emph{prescriptive} model. Finally, Ruiz et al.\ connect the predictive perspective with the prescriptive component, which is the central contribution of the present work.

Taken together, these studies show that football analytics is shaped by three recurring themes: the difficulty of the prediction task, the importance of feature design, and the need to align modelling choices with the actual decision-making objective. These observations provide the theoretical basis for the framework proposed in this thesis.

\section{Prescriptive Analytics in Football}
\label{sec:prescriptive_football}

Prescriptive analytics extends the role of data science from estimation to decision support. If predictive analytics answers the question \textit{what is likely to happen}, prescriptive analytics answers the question \textit{what should be done}. In broader analytics frameworks, predictive analytics is used to inform decisions, whereas prescriptive analytics is intended to suggest actions and clarify the likely consequences of alternative decision options~\cite{houtmeyers2021managing,bertsimas2020predictive}. In football, this distinction is especially important because coaches do not act as passive observers of statistical forecasts. They actively shape match outcomes through tactical choices, preparation strategies, and performance targets.

A model that outputs a probability of winning is useful, but incomplete. A coach also needs to know which tactical dimensions most strongly influence that probability, what the main risks are, and what practical adjustments could raise the team's chance of success. Existing football analytics literature, however, is strongly centred on forecasting match outcomes, classifier comparison, and feature effectiveness~\cite{rodrigues2022prediction,haruna2021predicting,kozak2021heterogeneous,tacspinar2021improvement}, and offers comparatively little support for the prescriptive question. In elite sports more generally, prescriptive analytics has been described as a decision-support layer built on top of other analytical steps, with the role of suggesting actions and supporting optimisation-oriented choices~\cite{houtmeyers2021managing}. In football specifically, prior work has shown that strategic and style-of-play features can have predictive value and can support recommendation-oriented tools for pre-game preparation~\cite{ruiz2017leicester}. In addition, recent football analytics research has demonstrated the usefulness of simulation-based frameworks for practical decision support in adjacent planning problems such as injury prevention and player availability~\cite{suresh2025sports}.

A particularly relevant family of methods for prescriptive analytics in football is the Monte Carlo simulation approach, in which a stochastic process is sampled many times in order to estimate quantities that are difficult to derive analytically. Two football-direct applications are particularly illustrative. Lahvi\v{c}ka~\cite{lahvicka2015importance} uses Monte Carlo simulation across twelve English Premier League seasons (combined with bookmaker odds) to quantify the importance of individual matches, defining match importance as the strength of the relationship between a single result and a given end-of-season outcome and producing estimates that align closely with earlier match-importance approaches while requiring no retrospective information. Jiao~\cite{jiao2022penalty} applies a Monte Carlo formulation to the simulation and optimisation of football penalty kicks, sampling shooting outcomes under five categories of goalkeeper behaviour using both uniform and normal random generators, with validation against 2021 English Premier League penalty data.

Two further references widen the methodological framing. Laviers~\cite{laviers2010playgen} proposes a Monte Carlo Tree Search (specifically Upper Confidence Bound Trees, UCT) approach for offensive play generation in American football. Although the application domain is gridiron rather than association football, the framework demonstrates that sampled rollouts can construct plausible plays in continuous-valued, multi-agent adversarial games, an idea that transfers directly to tactical search in soccer. From a more foundational perspective, Blanco et al.~\cite{blanco2021axioms} use a Bayesian Poisson regression structure (each scoreline is modelled as a draw from a Poisson distribution whose rate depends on home advantage and team-specific attack and defence parameters) applied to the 2020 Chilean Premier League, providing a probabilistic foundation that complements pure Monte Carlo sampling.

The closest precedent for this thesis's own epistemic stance is Piza Volio~\cite{piza2005futbol}, who develops a Monte Carlo model used during the 2001 CONCACAF qualifying tournament for the 2002 FIFA World Cup, simulating approximately $500{,}000$ tournament completions to estimate, for each round-robin match, dynamic win, draw, and loss probabilities that combine tournament form, home advantage, and FIFA ranking (alongside a smaller ``imponderability'' factor capturing already-qualified or already-eliminated states). The draw probability is anchored at an empirical baseline of $20.4\%$ (derived from analysis of thousands of historical World Cup matches) and dynamically clamped to the interval $[0.15, 0.25]$, while the home-win probability is a weighted combination of three terms (home-advantage proportion, tournament-performance ratio, and FIFA-ranking ratio) with weights $\tfrac{2}{5}, \tfrac{2}{5}, \tfrac{1}{5}$, reflecting the author's stated rule that the historical (FIFA-ranking) contribution is half as important as the other two. The qualification probabilities produced by the model were periodically updated and tracked publicly throughout the qualifying campaign. The author explicitly frames the framework as having ``more descriptive than predictive value'' in a sport as random as football, which is the same epistemic stance adopted in the present thesis. Across these different application contexts, the recurring idea is the same: Monte Carlo simulation transforms a probabilistic model into a flexible scenario generator, which is precisely the property exploited by the prescriptive framework proposed in this thesis.

The optimiser proposed in the present work fits naturally into this prescriptive-analytics perspective in which contextual data are used to recommend decision options rather than only to generate forecasts~\cite{houtmeyers2021managing,bertsimas2020predictive}. Recent work in professional football has also explored Monte Carlo-based simulation frameworks for decision support and probabilistic scenario analysis, although in a different application context focused on injury prevention and player availability rather than tactical prescription~\cite{suresh2025sports}. The detailed methodological design of the constrained tactical optimiser proposed in this thesis is described in Chapter~\ref{chap:approach}, together with the evolution from earlier rule-based heuristics through single-variable sweeps and manual tactical profiles up to the final Monte Carlo formulation.

\section{Machine Learning Framework for UmbraRo}
\label{sec:ml_framework}

The machine learning framework behind UmbraRo was designed to support all four pillars of the application: prediction, explanation, tactical prescription, and player-level selection. It is based on a dataset of approximately 1{,}600 Romanian Superliga matches covering five seasons, from 2020--2021 to 2024--2025. However, the main predictive modelling pipeline uses a strict chronological split: matches before July 1, 2024 are used for model development, while the 2024--2025 season is reserved as a holdout test period. This preserves the temporal realism of the evaluation and reflects the way such a system would be used in practice.

In this dataset, key variables include Elo ratings, which provide a dynamic estimate of team quality~\cite{elo1978rating}, and head-to-head context, which adds opponent-specific historical information. These ratings are complemented by other information such as the number of rest days and by rolling averages over the previous five matches for possession, shots, shots on target, corners, goals scored, and goals conceded. Together, these features aim to describe not only how strong a team is, but also how it has recently been playing. The importance of combining contextual and performance-based features is consistent with the football prediction literature~\cite{tacspinar2021improvement,haruna2021predicting,rodrigues2022prediction}.

The predictive component of the framework treats the problem as a binary classification task: \textit{Home Win} versus \textit{Not Home Win}. This formulation was selected after the multiclass setting proved less stable, largely due to the statistical ambiguity of draws. The binary structure made it easier to obtain reliable probability estimates and provided a more suitable basis for tactical optimisation. This direction is aligned with the broader literature, where formulation choices, feature design, and model family strongly affect football prediction quality~\cite{rodrigues2022prediction,kozak2021heterogeneous,tacspinar2021improvement}.

Model evaluation was performed using a rigorous time-aware methodology. Since football data is chronological, the system used rolling-origin validation instead of random train-test splits. This ensured that the models were always evaluated on future fixtures relative to their training data, which more closely matches a deployment scenario. In addition, preprocessing steps such as imputation and scaling were handled through Scikit-Learn pipelines in order to avoid data leakage. The project also evaluated calibration metrics, because the application depends on meaningful probability outputs rather than only on hard classifications.

A range of supervised classifiers were considered, of which Logistic Regression and CatBoost proved most informative for the goals of this thesis. Logistic Regression offered a simple and interpretable benchmark that produced strong pure predictive accuracy, which is consistent with prior football probability modelling work based on logistic regression~\cite{kollar2021predicting}. CatBoost~\cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost}, on the other hand, captured non-linear feature interactions more effectively and responded more smoothly inside the tactical optimisation loop, which made it the preferred prescriptive engine. The concrete experimental comparison of all considered classifiers is presented in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

A complementary, player-level layer was developed to address the \emph{selection} pillar. Whereas the outcome layer operates on team-level pre-match aggregates, the selection layer ingests per-90 player KPIs from Hudl Wyscout (minutes, passes, duels, shots, goals, defensive actions) and combines them with empirical-Bayes shrinkage toward positional means~\cite{brown2008empirical}, an availability score derived from the team's recent fixtures, and an opponent-style profile drawn from the same rolling-five tactical context used by the outcome layer. The eleven players selected for each fixture are obtained from a globally optimal one-to-one matching between players and formation slots via the Hungarian algorithm~\cite{kuhn1955hungarian}. The full methodology, the supervised lineup classifier trained pooled across all sixteen Romanian Superliga 2024-25 clubs, and the rolling-origin one-fixture-out validation are presented in Section~\ref{sec:xi}.

At the product level, the notebook outputs map directly to the application's main screens. Predictive probabilities become the match win probability modules on the Dashboard and the Match Intelligence sheet. Model explanation and feature influence become key drivers, risks, and contextual interpretation on the Match Intelligence sheet. The prescriptive optimiser becomes the Tactical Blueprint module, where the user sees baseline probability, optimised probability, uplift in percentage points, target tactical values, and the final AI tactical diagnosis. The starting eleven recommended by the player-level selection layer is rendered on a top-down pitch diagram inside the same screen. In this way, UmbraRo acts as the operational interface through which the theoretical and computational contributions of the thesis are delivered to coaching staff.

Therefore, the machine learning framework of this thesis should not be understood as an isolated backend experiment. It is the computational core of a complete football intelligence product that translates predictive and prescriptive analytics into a usable coaching system.
